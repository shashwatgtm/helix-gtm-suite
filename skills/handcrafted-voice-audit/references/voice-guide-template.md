# Voice guide template (blank)

The structure follows a practitioner's personal writing framework, the kind a B2B leader keeps so that every draft, by them or by a writer working for them, sounds like one person. It has eight sections: core expertise and themes; core writing personality and voice; content format guidelines; universal language characteristics; content generation process; things to avoid; quality benchmarks by format; evolution and adaptation. The headings of a writing style analysis (core personality, tone and voice, structural patterns, language, signature moves, patterns by format, audience adaptation) are folded into those eight sections.

A voice sample counts only if the writer wrote it. Before using a sample to set someone's voice, ask who wrote it; a draft written for them by someone else (a colleague, an agency or an AI tool) is not a sample of their voice and is not copied.

Every field starts blank. Fill a field only from the writer's own samples or answers, and put the evidence line (a quoted phrase from their sample, or "told me on [date]") next to it. Leave a field blank rather than guess. The skill supplies no tone mix, no percentages, no vocabulary, no framework names, no persona label and no sign off: those come from the writer or stay blank.

Fit to the channel: before filling, name the channel the guide is for and who writes in it (for example the writer's own LinkedIn posts, or a company blog written by freelancers for bank and insurer buyers). Reword every review line in sections 2, 5 and 7 to that channel ("article" instead of "post" for a blog, "freelancer" where someone else drafts), drop any line that does not apply and say so, and mark every line that is not drawn from the writer's samples or answers as "(template line, for you to confirm)". A template line never ships as the writer's own rule.

LinkedIn rule: for LinkedIn posts, these format rules are the guide's default and win over any general style preference recorded here: prose only (no bullets, bold or headings); no emoji, Unicode bold letters, arrows or checkmarks; no long or short dashes; and the banned words and patterns in banned-words-and-patterns.md. If the writer's samples use headings, bold, bullets or emoji in LinkedIn posts, record that as what the samples show and note the conflict; it enters the guide only if the writer decides to keep it, marked "(writer's decision)". Other channels (documents, email, articles) follow the writer's own samples.

- The reader's pain point: [blank]
- The ask or call to action: [blank]
- Whether two versions with different structure or emphasis are wanted: [blank]

Process steps
1. Confirm the inputs above; ask for any that are missing.
2. Anchor in a specific moment, test, tension or learning loop.
3. Flag performance energy and remove it.
4. Allow a partial piece when the loop is still open, instead of forcing a neat ending.
5. Do not polish away rawness if it adds clarity.
6. Raise a flag if the piece could become a longer piece, a series or a diagram.
7. Review for authenticity: read it as the writer's peer would.
